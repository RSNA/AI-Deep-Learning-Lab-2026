When to fine-tune

1
Prompt first. It is always the baseline: free and fast. In this lab the untrained model never followed the report format and named the body part correctly only 52% of the time.
2
Retrieve (RAG) when what is missing is text knowledge: guidelines, your protocols, prior reports.
3
Fine-tune when you need:
  • a new behavior or a fixed output format
  • a local visual skill (your scanners, your patients)
  • a smaller model that runs on your own servers
  • your department's house style
Our task needs a fixed format and a visual skill → fine-tune.
Clinical context · JulieLab 07 · RSNA 20268
Julie.

Data and labels decide the outcome

  • The label is the curriculum. The model learns your labels, including their mistakes.
  • Readers disagree. Label noise limits what any model can learn.
  • Shortcuts. In FracAtlas, hardware and fractures often appear together. Our model learned to call jewelry “hardware” and then also “fracture”.
  • Leakage. 68 validation images are exact copies of training images under other file names. The test set has none. Split by patient.
  • Subgroups. Check by body part, scanner, site and age. FracAtlas comes from three hospitals in one country.
  • Governance. An adapter trained on patient data can memorize it. Treat the weights as protected data.

Pick the metric for the task

85%
accuracy of a model that always answers “no fracture” on our test set. Only 63 of the 421 test X-rays show a fracture.
  • Yes/no questions → sensitivity, specificity, AUC
  • Short answers (body part, view) → exact match
  • Free-text reports → word-overlap scores (BLEU, ROUGE) miss clinical errors: “no acute fracture” and “acute fracture” share almost every word. Use clinical metrics that check the findings themselves (for example GREEN or RadGraph F1), plus a radiologist's review.

Would I trust this? A checklist

  1. Was it tested on images it never saw, from patients not in training?
  2. Is it right on the cases that matter (the dangerous miss), not just on average?
  3. Does it work on our scanners, our patients, our subgroups?
  4. What does it do with an image it should refuse (blank, wrong body part, not an X-ray)?
  5. Can I see why it answered (a probability, a heat map), and would I notice when it is wrong?

We come back to this list in Part 10.

CNN, LLM, VLM

CNN

image classifier

image in → fixed label out (“fracture: 0.83”). Answers only the question it was built for.

LLM

large language model

text in → text out. Cannot see the image.

VLM

vision-language model

image + text in → text out. One model, many questions. It can also hallucinate: write fluent text the image does not support.

Anatomy of a VLM

  • Vision encoder (a vision transformer): turns the image into a grid of feature vectors.
  • Projector: translates those features into tokens (“words”) the language model can read.
  • Language model: reads the image tokens and your question together, then writes the answer one token at a time.

Our model, Qwen3-VL-2B, has 2.1 billion weights. Part 3 of the notebook shows where they are.

Concepts · AliLab 07 · RSNA 202614
Ali.

How an X-ray becomes tokens

  • The image is cut into 16 × 16-pixel patches; every 2 × 2 block of patches becomes one token. One token = 32 × 32 pixels.
  • A 373 × 454-pixel X-ray → 168 tokens.
  • Twice the resolution = four times the tokens (and much more GPU memory).
  • We resize to 512 px: enough for body part and view, but it can hide a subtle fracture line. Resolution is a clinical trade-off.
Concepts · AliLab 07 · RSNA 202615
Ali.

What the model is graded on

  • Training = predict the next token. The model sees the image, the instruction and the correct report.
  • The error signal (loss) counts only the report tokens. We teach the answer, not how to repeat the question.

Why full fine-tuning does not fit a free GPU

Full fine-tuning keeps several extra numbers in GPU memory for every one of the 2.1 billion weights while it learns. LoRA trains less than 1% of the weights.

Concepts · AliLab 07 · RSNA 202618
Ali.

LoRA: a small, removable correction

  • The original weight matrix W stays frozen. Next to it we learn a correction B·A built from two thin matrices.
  • Only A and B are trained: 17.4 million weights, 0.8% of the model.
  • Saved as a ~70 MB file (an adapter) you can switch on and off, one per task or per site.

Knobs and failure modes

Learning rate matters most; more data keeps helping; rank matters less
% of the 63 test fractures found
Overfitting: too many passes over too few images. Training loss falls while validation loss rises.
Forgetting: the model can lose skills it had before, such as describing an image.
Hallucination: the language habit wins over the image (slide 6).

Take-home messages

  1. A VLM = vision encoder + projector + language model. LoRA trains less than 1% of the weights and fits on a free GPU.
  2. Format is learned in minutes; content needs data. Fracture detection kept improving up to all 3,258 images.
  3. Data quality decides the outcome. Split by patient, look for duplicates, know your class balance.
  4. Accuracy can lie. Report sensitivity, specificity and AUC; pick the operating point for the clinical use.
  5. Probe for failure. Hallucination, shortcuts and forgetting do not show up in one accuracy number.
